The universe spans 46 orders of magnitude — from quantum foam smaller than a proton to cosmic filaments billions of light-years wide. Scroll to traverse it all.

Category: Quantum Foam
10⁻³⁵ m
Planck Length
The absolute smallest meaningful unit of length in physics. Below this scale, our understanding of space and time completely breaks down. Space itself may be quantized — grainy like pixels — at this level.
If a proton were the size of the observable universe, the Planck length would be... about the size of a proton.
Category: Subatomic
10⁻¹⁸ m
Quark
Quarks are the fundamental building blocks of protons and neutrons. They come in six "flavors" — up, down, charm, strange, top, and bottom — and are held together by the strong nuclear force via gluons. A quark has never been observed in isolation.
Quarks are so tightly bound that pulling them apart would create new quarks from the energy used — a phenomenon called "confinement."
Category: Subatomic
10⁻¹⁵ m
Proton
Each proton is made of two "up" quarks and one "down" quark, bound by the strong force. The nucleus of every hydrogen atom is a single proton, making it the simplest nuclear structure in the universe.
If a proton were the size of a football stadium, the electrons orbiting it would be specks of dust 50 km away — atoms are mostly empty space.
Category: Atomic
10⁻¹⁰ m
Atom
The atom — from the Greek "atomos" meaning indivisible — is the basic unit of matter. A dense nucleus of protons and neutrons is surrounded by a cloud of electrons. Everything you can see or touch is made of these tiny structures.
A human hair is about 1 million carbon atoms wide. There are more atoms in a grain of sand than stars in the observable universe.
Category: Nanoscale
10⁻⁷ m
Virus
Viruses occupy a haunting middle ground — not quite alive, not quite inert. They are genetic code wrapped in protein, hijacking living cells to replicate. COVID-19 is about 100 nanometers. Bacteria are typically 10–100× larger.
You could line up 1,000 coronavirus particles across the width of a human hair.
Category: Cellular
10⁻⁵ m
Human Cell
The basic unit of life. A typical human cell is about 10 micrometers — visible under a light microscope. Inside each of your ~37 trillion cells is about 2 meters of DNA, packed into a nucleus smaller than a speck of dust.
If all the DNA in your body were stretched out end to end, it would reach the Sun and back about 300 times.
Category: Human Scale
~1.7 m
Human Being
You. Right here. A collection of 37 trillion cells, 206 bones, approximately 86 billion neurons, all working in concert. The universe needed 13.8 billion years of cosmic evolution to produce a being complex enough to contemplate its own scale.
You are simultaneously unimaginably large compared to a quark and incomprehensibly tiny compared to a galaxy.
Category: Planetary
12,742 km
Earth
Our home — a rocky, water-covered world orbiting a medium star. Earth's diameter is 12,742 km, yet its entire atmosphere is a thin skin less than 1% of that width. From the Moon, you can cover the entire Earth with your thumb.
If Earth were shrunk to the size of a billiard ball, it would actually be smoother than the ball — Mount Everest would be barely detectable.
Category: Stellar
1,392,700 km
The Sun
Our star — a massive sphere of plasma undergoing nuclear fusion at its core, converting 4 million tons of mass into energy every second. The Sun contains 99.86% of the solar system's total mass. 1.3 million Earths could fit inside it.
The light reaching your eyes right now left the Sun 8 minutes and 20 seconds ago. The energy itself took 100,000 years to travel from the Sun's core to its surface.
Category: Planetary System
~1.4×10¹³ km
Solar System
The Sun and everything gravitationally bound to it: 8 planets, 5 dwarf planets, hundreds of moons, millions of asteroids, and billions of comets. The outer edge (the heliopause) is where the solar wind stops — about 120 AU from the Sun.
The Voyager 1 probe was launched in 1977 and only crossed into interstellar space in 2012. At 17 km/s, it still takes tens of thousands of years to reach the nearest star.

Category: Interstellar
4.24 light-years
Proxima Centauri
The nearest star to our Sun — Proxima Centauri, part of the Alpha Centauri triple system. A red dwarf star smaller and dimmer than our Sun. At the current speed of our fastest spacecraft, it would take about 75,000 years to reach it.
If the Sun were a basketball in New York City, Proxima Centauri would be another basketball in Los Angeles.
Category: Galactic
100,000 ly
Milky Way Galaxy
Our home galaxy — a barred spiral containing 200–400 billion stars, at least as many planets, and a supermassive black hole (Sagittarius A*) at its center with the mass of 4 million suns. Our solar system is about 26,000 light-years from the center.
If the Milky Way were shrunk to the size of the continental United States, our solar system would be about the size of a single quarter.

Category: Intergalactic
2.5 million ly
Andromeda Galaxy
Our nearest large galactic neighbor, visible to the naked eye as a faint smudge. Andromeda is on a collision course with the Milky Way — but don't panic. The merger will begin in about 4.5 billion years, and because galaxies are mostly empty space, few stars will actually collide.
Andromeda contains about 1 trillion stars — roughly twice as many as our Milky Way.
Category: Galaxy Group
~10 million ly
The Local Group
The cluster of galaxies that includes our Milky Way, Andromeda, and about 54 smaller satellite galaxies. All are gravitationally bound and will eventually merge into one massive elliptical galaxy in the far future.
The Large Magellanic Cloud — a dwarf galaxy orbiting the Milky Way — is slowly being consumed by our galaxy's gravity.
Category: Cosmic
93 billion ly
The Observable Universe
The sphere of space visible from Earth — limited by the speed of light and the age of the universe. It contains an estimated 2 trillion galaxies, each with hundreds of billions of stars. And this is only what we can observe — the actual universe is almost certainly far, far larger.
The most distant light we can observe — the Cosmic Microwave Background — is a snapshot of the universe just 380,000 years after the Big Bang, when atoms first formed.

You are made of stardust.

Every atom in your body heavier than hydrogen was forged in the heart of a dying star. You are the universe experiencing itself. From the Planck length to the cosmic horizon — it is all connected, and you are part of it.
